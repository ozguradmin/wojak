Titanic's lookouts had no binoculars. Was a key to blame?

On the night of 14 April 1912, the Titanic's two lookouts, Frederick Fleet and Reginald Lee, watched from the crow's nest without binoculars. Fleet told the US Senate inquiry that the lookouts had a pair between Belfast and Southampton, and that when they asked for glasses in Southampton, they were told there were none for them.

A popular explanation involves Second Officer David Blair, who was taken off the ship in a last-minute reshuffle of officers at Southampton. Reportedly, he left with the key to a crow's nest locker still in his pocket, possibly by mistake. A key linked to him sold at auction in 2007 for £90,000. But the story is disputed: some sources say the key belonged to the crow's nest telephone box rather than a binocular locker, and a crew member told the British inquiry that Blair had asked him to lock the glasses away in Blair's own cabin.

Asked at the Senate whether glasses would have helped, Fleet said they "could have seen it a bit sooner," enough "to get out of the way." The British inquiry, however, found binoculars were not necessary and blamed the ship's excessive speed; several senior seamen testified that lookouts were not meant to use them. The final frame is the report's own answer to its question 11 on binoculars.
